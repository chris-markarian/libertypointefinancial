In 2021, Congress updated the interest-rate assumptions used to test whether a policy qualifies as life insurance under the tax code. Policies issued since then can be designed differently than policies issued before. That doesn’t make older policies worse. It does mean many owners have never had their policy’s structure, funding, and performance explained to them. A review covers how your policy is set up today and what your options are, and for many people the conclusion is to keep their current policy.

What changed in 2021

This section is general education about the tax code, not tax or legal advice. Your tax professional can tell you how these rules apply to you.

The 7702 tests

Section 7702 of the Internal Revenue Code sets the tests a contract must meet to be treated as life insurance for federal tax purposes. Among other things, the tests limit how much premium can go into a policy relative to its death benefit.

New interest-rate assumptions

The Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 replaced the fixed minimum interest rates used in those tests (previously 4% and 6%) with lower rates that can adjust over time (2% and 4% for 2021). The related “7-pay” test for modified endowment contracts was updated too.

Who it applies to

The changes generally apply to policies issued after December 31, 2020. Policies issued earlier generally keep the rules in place when they were issued, although certain material changes to an existing policy can affect which rules apply. That’s one reason to get professional and tax guidance before changing a policy.

What this does and doesn’t mean. Newer rules allow some newly issued policies to be designed with a different balance of premium and death benefit. They don’t make policies issued before 2021 inferior, and replacing a policy is not guaranteed to produce better results. Whether any change makes sense depends on your age, health, goals, current policy costs and surrender charges.

What a policy review looks at

A review is a plain-English walkthrough of the policy you already own. You’ll leave knowing more about it, whatever you decide.

How your policy is set up

  • Death benefit option and amount
  • Premium schedule and funding level
  • Owner, insured and beneficiary arrangement
  • Riders you’re paying for

How it’s performing

  • Cash value versus the original illustration
  • Changes to caps and participation rates since issue
  • Cost of insurance and policy charges
  • Loan balances and lapse risk, if any

Your options

  • Keep the policy as is (a common outcome)
  • Adjust premium or death benefit within the policy
  • Request an updated in-force illustration from your carrier
  • Understand, but not commit to, any alternatives

Common questions

Is there a cost?

No. The review is free and there is no obligation to buy anything or make any change.

Will I be asked to replace my policy?

Not necessarily, and many reviews end with keeping the current policy. If an alternative is ever discussed, replacing a policy can involve surrender charges, new underwriting, a new contestability period, and different costs, and state replacement rules require specific disclosures. You decide what, if anything, to do.

Does the 2021 change affect the policy I already have?

Generally, the updated rules apply to policies issued after December 31, 2020. Certain material changes to an older policy can affect how the rules apply, which is why it’s worth understanding your policy before changing it. Please consult a tax professional about your specific situation.

What should I have ready?

Your most recent annual policy statement is the most useful document. If you have your original illustration or a recent in-force illustration, bring that too. Please don’t enter policy numbers, account numbers or Social Security numbers on this website.
